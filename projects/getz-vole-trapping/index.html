The Getz Vole Trapping Dataset

For twenty-five years, Lowell Getz and his crews live-trapped wild prairie voles and meadow voles every month in alfalfa, bluegrass, and tallgrass prairie fields outside Urbana, Illinois. Every animal was individually marked, so each record says who was caught, in which trap, and when. This page lets you explore those records directly.

How the study worked

Voles were never kept in enclosures. The study fields were open and unfenced, so animals were born, moved in, moved out, and died on their own. Getz's crews sampled them by setting live-traps on a fixed grid, marking every animal, and releasing it where it was caught.

GridA field plot, 0.5–2 ha, with traps at fixed points 10 m apart. Getz ran 19 numbered grids in three habitats, but usually only one to three per habitat at a time (timeline below).
StationOne trap at one fixed point on a grid. Station numbers are Getz's own codes; the map rebuilds where each one sat.
SessionOne monthly round of trapping on a grid: traps set for three days and checked six times (about 8:00 and 15:00). Every active grid got one session each month.
Check & captureA check is one visit to every trap. A capture is one animal found in one trap on one check. Animals were released each time, so one vole can be captured several times in a session.
Nest captureAlfalfa II–III only, 1982–87. For a social-behavior study, traps also went around every known nest each Monday–Tuesday and Thursday–Friday. One nest capture means the animal was caught at least once in that two-day period.
Known aliveDensity counts an animal as present in every session between its first and last capture, even ones it skipped (the "minimum number known alive").

Example: "15 captures in 6 sessions" means the animal was trapped 15 times across 6 different months, about 2–3 times in each three-day session.

Where and when: the 19 grids

Grids sat in two places 6 km northeast of Urbana: the university's Phillips Tract and Trelease Prairie. Alfalfa fields were replanted as they aged, so the five alfalfa grids are mostly successive fields, not parallel ones. Some grid numbers re-use the same ground: bluegrass grid 6 was later split into 9, 10, and 11. Social life: who shares a trap

Getz's wooden traps could hold several voles at once, so two voles found in the same trap on the same check were physically together. Prairie voles are famous for male–female pair bonds, while meadow voles are usually described as solitary. Here the two species are side by side, with males in blue and females in red.

Heavier males, more females?

Do pairs stay together?

Male–female pairs where both animals were caught again the next month: how often they were caught together again. They are compared with neighbors (pairs that shared a trap but weren't caught together) and with any male–female pair on the same grid.

Coming back to the trap

Being live-trapped should be stressful, so you might expect voles to learn to avoid traps. They didn't. They came back to the same traps, and were caught more often, not less, as they gained experience. Small-mammal trappers call this being "trap-happy"; a dry wooden box with cracked corn inside is a good deal for a vole. Only grids with exact station layouts are used here.

Breeding calendar

Each cell is one month on the unmanipulated grids: the share of females caught that were pregnant (judged by palpation), and the share of males in breeding condition (testes descended). Blank cells had fewer than five animals of that sex.

Body mass

Every capture on the monthly grids was weighed to the nearest gram. Each animal counts at most once per session, using its average mass that session, so frequently trapped animals aren't over-counted. These are descriptive summaries of the raw records, not fitted models.

About the data

Trapping protocol in detail

Each grid had one wooden multiple-capture live-trap per station, 10 m apart. Each month, traps were prebaited with cracked corn for two days, then set in the afternoon and checked at about 08:00 and 15:00 on each of the next three days. Voles were toe-clipped at first capture for identification, and at every capture the crew recorded species, animal number, station, sex, reproductive condition, and body mass. Some grids were experimental: one species was removed every month, or the grid got supplemental food (rabbit chow in a bottle at every station). The twice-weekly nest trapping (Alfalfa II–III, March 1982 to May 1987) put four or five traps around each known nest from 06:30 Monday to 09:30 Tuesday and again Thursday to Friday. Getz estimated it caught more than 99% of the animals present.

How this explorer processes the files
  • Source: Getz's 38 "original field trap records" files (one per species per grid), which he had already cleaned, plus the two twice-weekly Alfalfa data files. The only further changes are noted below.
  • An individual is a species + grid + animal number. Getz re-used number sequences over the years, so if the same number goes uncaught on a grid for more than 300 days, it is treated as a new animal.
  • Nest captures are linked to a grid animal with the same number when they fall within 60 days of that animal's grid captures. Recorded sex agrees for 95% of linked animals. Unlinked numbers become "nest-only" animals, marked with an n in their ID.
  • Capture dates more than 30 days from the rest of their session (about 50 typos) are replaced with the session's median date.
  • Density is the minimum number known alive. The 25-year means it gives match Getz et al. (2001): bluegrass prairie voles 19.6/ha vs. 18 published, bluegrass meadow voles 13.5 vs. 14, tallgrass prairie voles 8.0 vs. 8.
  • Animals taken off removal grids are counted in the totals but not drawn as lifelines.
  • Trap checks are coded morning (AM) or afternoon (PM). A third, undocumented code appears only in Alfalfa II and III from 1982 to 1987, the years of the social-behavior study, whose grid sessions added a 20:00 check. It is shown here as "Eve", probably that evening check.
  • Grid positions are rebuilt from Getz's station codes and scanned grid maps. Alfalfa and the Phillips Tract grids are exact. The Trelease Prairie grid (an irregular L shape) and the BG Control grid, which changed layout twice, are approximate.
